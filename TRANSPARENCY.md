## 2. Which network, which assets

Everything runs on Base mainnet, chain id 8453. It is not a testnet: every coin, every asset a coin is quoted in and every fee is real value, and your wallet should show chain 8453 on every request.

A coin is quoted in a tokenized stock or a crypto asset. A tokenized stock is one of Coinbase’s stock tokens on Base, a permissioned asset issued by Coinbase, not a share in the company. Coinbase can pause a stock token’s transfers, stop an address from sending or receiving it through its transfer policies (held in Base’s policy registry, [`0x8453000000000000000000000000000000000002`](https://basescan.org/address/0x8453000000000000000000000000000000000002)), and take the balance of an address it has blocked. A paused stock freezes every pool quoted in it and the dividends paid in it; a blocked dividend contract cannot pay out until the block lifts. Pines has no control over any of this and no relationship with Coinbase beyond building on Base.

One stock token is not always one share: after a split or a similar corporate action Coinbase applies a multiplier to the token, so the number of tokens and the shares they stand for can differ. The prices on this site are per token, as the token’s price feed reports them.

A crypto asset is one of 11 ERC-20 tokens on Base (since October 20, 2026), each with its own issuer, whose powers Pines cannot override either. ETH is quoted as WETH, its wrapped form, because a pool needs a token. What each issuer can do, read from the token’s verified contract:

The ZIP’s SHA-256 is `8355b5fe8d3822e4d90908b80254f84324f92a98e503799de90f4a97ab6d36a8`. Its `build.json` names the two commits of Pines’ private development repository it was built and packaged from; the public repository is a path-for-path export of that repository, and its published extension sources follow the site-served ZIP’s source snapshot, with four internal comment attributions removed; the public README describes those comment-only omissions. The version number is set by the release packaging, not by `build.mjs`, and the package cannot yet be rebuilt byte for byte from the published source. An unpacked copy runs from the folder you extract and never updates on its own, and it cannot be loaded beside the store’s copy, which has the same id. Its permissions are `offscreen`, `storage`, `sidePanel`, `alarms`, `webRequest`, `proxy`. It requests `webRequestAuthProvider` when you consent to Grok routing. It asks for access to a provider’s site (`https://chatgpt.com/*`, `https://claude.ai/*`, `https://grok.com/*`) only when a verification needs it, from the side panel, and you can withdraw that access in Chrome at any time. It has no content scripts and loads no remote code, and its content security policy lets it open connections to `https://id.pines.family` and `https://grok.com` for the routing check.

Only pages on these origins can talk to it: `https://app.pines.family`, `https://front.pines.family`, `https://pines.family`, `http://localhost:5270`, `http://localhost:5180`, `http://127.0.0.1:5180`. Chrome’s manifest patterns cannot name a port, so its `externally_connectable` admits `https://app.pines.family/*`, `https://front.pines.family/*`, `https://pines.family/*`, `http://localhost/*`, `http://127.0.0.1/*`; the extension itself then answers only a top-level, non-incognito page whose exact origin is in that list. A page anywhere else cannot call it.

With your permission it opens the provider in a tab and uses Chrome’s native request events to capture the session headers needed by the selected schema. It retains only the permitted headers in memory. Those headers are your session credentials: whoever holds them can act as you on that provider until the session ends. The extension’s published code is what keeps them from Pines; its content security policy alone cannot do that because it allows connections to `https://id.pines.family`, Pines’ own API. While the site permission is granted, Chrome lets the extension observe request metadata on that site; the code selects the schema’s capture trigger and discards unrelated data, and the permission can be removed afterwards.

After you consent, the extension checks the enclave and opens an encrypted HPKE channel to it. TLSNotary’s WebAssembly prover (0.1.0-alpha.15) replays the permitted provider requests in Proxy mode. Outgoing Authorization, complete Cookie and other secret request header values stay hidden in the proof. For ordinary subscription checks, response header values also stay hidden, except Content-Type, Content-Length, Transfer-Encoding and Content-Encoding. Header names and lengths remain visible. The Grok email check reveals both complete responses, including their headers, inside its separately admitted enclave; its outgoing Cookie and exact User-Agent stay hidden. The enclave authenticates and decodes the response body, checks the plan and returns a signed result. The extension checks that result and its account-confirmation MAC. A failed attestation or identity check stops disclosure.

It requests only the listed account or subscription endpoint, not conversation or file endpoints. The response can still include personal or billing details beyond the plan fields.

## 4. The enclave, and what it proves

The response is read in one place: an AWS Nitro Enclave, an isolated virtual machine with no disk, no interactive access and no network of its own, on an AWS EC2 host in the Sydney region (ap-southeast-2). Its only connections run through that host: the encrypted channel to your browser, and fixed routes to the three providers. Pines runs the host, which relays encrypted bytes and cannot read the enclave’s memory; Railway, which runs Pines’ API, relays the same encrypted bytes.

AWS’s hardware signs a measurement of the code the enclave booted: PCR0 (the image), PCR1 (its kernel and boot) and PCR2 (its application). Ordinary subscription checks disclose only to this measurement, which is compiled into the extension: PCR0 `2ced2fc3326c32d4438e048bc23f537e65cfb4391d7b1bc02a4f9c268ed892d2b4be474af2128277eb54aa8041a81224`, PCR1 `4b4d5b3661b3efc12920900c80e126e4ce783c522de6c02a2a5bf7af3a2b9327b86776f188e4be1c1c404a129dbda493`, PCR2 `e8330b01d8992ee72bebf6c17e815badcbf2af342d3ae15c1ecd37295506df52c1f53f5f5e2a3baa5d42d6adbdc957aa`.

Before each verification the extension checks, in your browser, that the attestation chains to AWS’s Nitro root certificate, was made within the last 60 seconds for this attempt, comes from an enclave not running in debug mode, carries exactly those three values, and vouches for the channel key and for the key that will sign the result. Pines’ API checks the same attestation independently. Neither the page nor the API can change what the extension expects.

What that proves, if you trust the extension you installed and AWS: the disclosed provider response becomes readable inside an enclave running the admitted image, under a key made for your attempt. Pines’ web page, its API and the host relaying the traffic cannot read that response or turn the attestation check off. The image decides which derived values leave it; an email check also sends the address transiently to the API for delivery.

What the image does is public. A measurement identifies code without describing it, so the source every file of the image is built from is published at https://github.com/PinesFamily/pines-verifier, and `tools/tee-native/reproduce/gm1026-enclave.py` rebuilds the image from it, twice and from clean, and compares the result with the three values above. Rebuilt that way, it matches them. Reading that source is how you check that the enclave reads the response, signs the short result the next section lists and keeps nothing else; the extension’s own check is what ties the image you read to the one your browser talks to.

The extension is published differently. The sources of 1.20.3 are in the same repository for review, and the two binaries it loads — the attestation validator and TLSNotary’s prover — rebuild there byte for byte, but its package does not yet: what runs is the folder you installed, and the package’s SHA-256 above is what identifies it. Its `config.js` carries the PCR values above in plain text, so you can read what your copy pins.

Which measurement the extension accepts is no longer compiled into it. The identity publishes the admitted set as a signed document, `pines-trust-manifest-v1`, at https://id.pines.family/trust-manifest: the admitted enclave images (each with the public build commit that reproduces it), the live schema per provider and the retired ones still readable, and the revocation lists. The extension fetches it when it starts and before each verification, checks its `ECDSA-P256-SHA256` signature against two owner public keys compiled into the package (`pines-owner-active-20261020` and `pines-owner-standby-20261020`, active and standby), refuses a version below the last one it accepted, and proves against nothing else; this site checks the same document with the same keys and holds the identity’s published capability to it. What still needs a new package: the owner keys themselves, the protocol version, the attestation validator and the Nitro root, and a new provider host. The signed manifest is published with the source, so each admitted image can be rebuilt from the commit it names.

Also outside the guarantee: AWS itself — its hardware, its hypervisor and the keys that sign attestations; the traffic metadata Pines’ servers see — when you verify, how much is sent and from which address; availability, since Pines can stop the host; and anyone other than you, because the attestation is checked off-chain (see “What a claim puts on chain”). To check a session yourself, open the developer tools of the extension’s offscreen page from `chrome://extensions` during a verification: the attestation arrives in the response to `/tee/admission` as the hex string `offer.evidence.cose`, and any Nitro attestation verifier can check it against [AWS’s published root certificate](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/enclaves/latest/user/verify-root.html).

## 6. What a proof reveals, per provider

Each ordinary subscription proof authenticates one HTTPS exchange with the provider. Native selective disclosure hides request credentials and response header values other than content and transfer framing, including Set-Cookie and opaque provider headers. The full response body is revealed inside the enclave, where it is decoded and checked. Body fields can still contain personal or billing information. These are the three ordinary schemas, each identified by a digest you can compare with the live policy.

- ChatGPT — pines.chatgpt.tee.native v6 (sha256:cd5e6039d98ecc181ca5f965515cf6eaa5a8dbc621ecd7673c492143f5fe223d): GET https://chatgpt.com/backend-api/wham/usage. Judged: plan_type (Plus, Pro, Pro Lite or Pro Max) and user_id (the account). Hidden from the enclave and from Pines: outgoing authorization, the complete outgoing cookie value, other secret request header values and all response header values except content and transfer framing. Also revealed to the enclave: the full authenticated JSON body, including any personal fields, the request user-agent and response framing. Decoded JSON body limit: 16 KiB.
- Claude — pines.claude.tee.native v3 (sha256:b8209443cbf6aa759e9bef8b808de111e0de7837773612facb7387f4726738e2): GET https://claude.ai/api/organizations/{org_uuid}. Judged: the personal organisation’s uuid (the account), its rate_limit_tier and capabilities (Pro or Max). Hidden from the enclave and from Pines: outgoing session cookie values, other secret request header values and all response header values except content and transfer framing. Also revealed to the enclave: the selected organisation’s full JSON body, including its name, the request user-agent and response framing. Decoded JSON body limit: 12 KiB.
- Grok — pines.grok.tee.native v3 (sha256:72127c492d778072c103b374177cc6d71f60e0a49ccac7aa1b23fb6da80aff75): GET https://grok.com/rest/subscriptions?status=SUBSCRIPTION_STATUS_ACTIVE. Judged: xaiUserId (the account), tier (a paid plan) and status, which must be SUBSCRIPTION_STATUS_ACTIVE. Hidden from the enclave and from Pines: outgoing session cookie values, other secret request header values and all response header values except content and transfer framing. Also revealed to the enclave: the active subscription response’s full JSON body, including billing fields, the request user-agent and response framing. Decoded JSON body limit: 8 KiB.

The enclave reads the whole JSON body in memory to decide the plan, then signs a short result: the plan, a domain-separated keyed pseudonym of the account id, your wallet, the provider, the attempt, the enclave’s measurement and the byte counts of the exchange. Beside the result, the enclave signs a separate facts record with the same key, bound to that result by its hash, its attempt and its session, carrying “values”, the account facts its schema discloses — Grok email: when the subscription began, when the account was created, the billing platform, a session-salted commitment to the e-mail address instead of the address. The result itself keeps the fields above, and only the result is sent to the signer of claims. Pines’ API receives that result — never the response, the raw account id or, from a ChatGPT or Claude check, the email. For the ordinary schemas, the encoded body is capped at 3.5 KiB; gzip and identity encoding are accepted within the decoded limits above. The response must be a 200 with JSON.

The enclave reads the whole JSON body because the plan check also needs the account identity and the provider’s surrounding data structure. The response body stays inside it. Hiding response headers does not hide fields inside that body.

For an ordinary subscription proof, the provider sees your browser visit followed by one GET of the account endpoint above with your session credential, arriving from Pines’ AWS host. The enclave checks the exact GET and empty request body. For the Grok email check, the provider sees the two selected GETs and the browser’s routed Grok traffic from the same fixed relay address. The verification requests do not write to your account.

Why Proxy mode: TLSNotary offers a verifier two ways to take part. On 2026-09-25 Pines chose Proxy mode, with no custom circuits or extra proof systems. In Proxy mode the verifier relays the TLS connection and sees its encrypted traffic, instead of running a joint computation over the whole handshake, which keeps the work light enough for a browser tab within the time above. Its assumption, stated plainly: the path from the verifier to the provider runs through Pines’ host, and whoever controls that path, working with someone proving, could feed the enclave a response the provider never sent. That is a question about whether a verification is authentic. A separate signing enclave does not remove this verification assumption; the legacy path also trusts its hosted attestor key, as “What a claim puts on chain” says. It is not a question about confidentiality: the host relays only encrypted bytes and cannot read what you disclose.

The site calls this zkTLS. The zero-knowledge part of the proof is what lets the enclave check the exchange without learning your credentials or the hidden header values. The response body is revealed to the enclave in full, so what keeps it from Pines is the enclave, not a zero-knowledge proof.

## 7. What Pines keeps

From a successful verification, the durable record holds: the provider and the schema id and version, your wallet address, when it was verified and when it expires, the one plan value, and a salted one-way identifier of the account. The enclave computes an HMAC over the canonical provider and account identity using one stable Sydney KMS-backed key held by a native helper. The API derives its identifier from that pseudonym with a secret salt. That is deliberate: the pseudonym is keyed by provider and account, not by wallet, so one subscription cannot feed many wallets; it follows that the wallets one account verified are linkable to each other in Pines’ records for as long as that key is the one in use. On chain the nullifier is salted per launch and links nothing. The raw account id, the email from a ChatGPT or Claude check, the response body, the headers and the cookies never reach Pines’ servers.

When a provider’s check includes the account’s e-mail address (a Grok check does), Pines’ API receives that address with the enclave’s result and uses it once: to send a 6-digit code that works for 10 minutes, through Resend, with a link the mailbox’s owner can use to block the verification. The verification is not saved until the code is entered, so someone holding only a copy of your provider session cannot complete one, and you are told when someone tries. The API holds the address in memory while the code can be entered and writes it to no database row, log or metric. Resend’s standard plans retain sent-message content, recipients and delivery logs for 30 days. Open and click tracking are off for our sending domain. It keeps the code’s state and a keyed hash of the code for 7 days, and, to limit how many codes one address receives, a keyed hash of the address that changes every hour and every day and is deleted when that window ends. A blocked verification stops, and the wallet that asked for it goes on the operator’s quarantine list; the provider account itself is not blocked, so its owner can still verify it. A ChatGPT or Claude check does not include the address, so it sends no code and is saved without one.

For the signing-enclave path, the API also keeps the complete signed ticket, boot offer, grant and verification receipt encrypted with an independent AES-256-GCM key. The plaintext pseudonym is needed transiently for signing checks, but is not retained in database records, logs or browser responses. Access requires the matching recipient and an unexpired, trusted record. Explicit deletion removes the live encrypted evidence; expiry and revocation purge it. Backups and database history can retain ciphertext until their retention and key-retirement policies remove access. Deletion does not erase public claims or an account-binding obligation.

Per attempt, for rate limiting and support: your wallet, the admission ticket, the enclave’s attestation and the signed grant, a status and error code, a hash of the enclave’s signed result, a keyed one-way hash of your IP address (HMAC-SHA256 under a server secret, so it cannot be reversed by trying every address without that secret), and timestamps. Attempt rows are retained for about 7 days.

Per attempt, to spot coordinated abuse, the API also keeps the connection’s facts: the country and the network (the autonomous system number) it comes from, resolved from your IP address on Pines’ own server against MaxMind’s GeoLite2 databases, so no lookup leaves that server; a keyed one-way hash of the address (HMAC-SHA256 under the same server secret, an IPv6 address by its /64 network), never the address itself; a coarse browser class (browser, major version and operating system); the Pines Verifier version it reports; the time each step took; and the outcome. They are kept for 90 days and then deleted. Pines’ metrics count them in buckets only: no address, hash, wallet or account is ever a label. This product includes GeoLite2 data created by MaxMind, available from https://www.maxmind.com.

The identifier binds the account to your wallet for 30 days, so a subscription cannot feed an unlimited number of wallets, while successful verification has no wallet count limit.

Separately, as with any web service, request logs record the IP address and user agent of each request to the API and the media service. Railway, which hosts them, keeps those logs for 30 days. Pines’ own monitoring keeps server logs and browser error reports for 30 days; the server logs it collects from Railway carry no IP address. The Privacy Policy gives the same figures.

Operations see a verification trace with a closed set of fields — identifiers, the schema, the assigned verifier, states, fixed error codes and timings — and never provider facts, receipt records, tickets, disclosed bytes or addresses. Transcripts are not stored anywhere.

The records live in a managed PostgreSQL database in the United States. Operators can read minimal record metadata; complete saved evidence requires the separately held encryption key. Legacy claim keys and the identity salt remain service secrets. New claim signing keys are generated inside the separate signing enclave and do not leave it. None of these secrets belongs on the machines that serve this site.

In your browser, the site uses local storage for the wallet you last connected, the filters you chose, a claim in progress, and the reference to your latest verification of each subscription with its expiry — the receipt a claim presents instead of a wallet signature, and only for the wallet that verified. Analytics, where enabled, are cookieless page counts proxied through this domain; browser error reports carry no wallet address and no identifier that follows you.

## 8. What a claim puts on chain

Saving a verification does not submit a claim or authorize a wallet transaction. A later claim request presents that saved verification; the identity service applies its current claim policy and the drop’s eligibility rules before signing. Sponsorship, when enabled, can then pay for a qualifying first claim without a wallet transaction approval.

When you claim, a signing enclave signs a four-field envelope for this recipient, chain and admitted clone. The contract verifier checks the enrolled signer and current image, schema and generation eligibility. The hosted identity service checks the response independently before it exposes a durable claim authorization.

The attestation names your wallet, the launch’s claim contract, the public subject identifier, plan, verification image and schema, verification time and a per-clone nullifier. Public subject identifiers can link claims for the same provider account. The permanent registry limits successful claims per wallet and prevents the same subject from claiming a clone twice.

Claimed coins arrive staked in the launch’s dividend distributor and earn from the first trade; leaving a staked claim has a 72-hour cooldown. You pay the claim’s gas in ETH on chain 8453.

The optional second signature uses the same digest and never replaces the enclave signature. When the shared registry requires it, an unavailable or revoked co-signer stops issuance and execution. Activation asks for no extra wallet signature. The co-key is hosted separately; its custody, operators and hosting provider remain additional trust assumptions.

The signing-enclave path still trusts AWS Nitro attestation, the accepted verifier and signing images, their implementation, the verification network assumptions and owner-controlled registry policy. The owner may change the optional co-signer role and requirement immediately; this exception does not remove the delayed image and schema activation rules. An owner or infrastructure compromise can disrupt service, and accepted verification evidence can still be false if its upstream assumptions fail.

## 9. What your wallet is asked to sign

Verifying and claiming ask for no message. A verification binds its receipt to the selected wallet, and the browser that verified keeps that receipt reference for later claims. A self-paid claim is sent by that wallet; a sponsored claim is sent by a relayer and can pay only the recipient fixed by the attestor. One message remains, signed as plain text (EIP-191), and only to use a saved verification in a browser that did not make it — another device, or after clearing this site’s data. Its text is exactly this, with the bracketed values filled in:

- Pines verification wallet sign-in
- Application: pines
- Chain ID: 8453
- Wallet: [your address]
- Verifier: [the verifier release the policy names]
- Nonce: [a one-time challenge]
- Expires: [a few minutes from now]
- Authorize verification and receipt access for this wallet for 30 minutes. This does not authorize a transaction.

It proves you control the wallet for 30 minutes of verification and receipt access and authorises nothing on chain; a wallet shows it as a message, not a transaction.

Transactions, each shown by your wallet before it is sent: `approve` — an exact amount of the asset a coin is quoted in to the dev-buy router `0xd275EEc13868Cae985c5decb67EB5e46D27cd35D` when you launch, and the exact amount you stake of a coin to that launch’s own distributor, when the allowance you have already given it does not cover the stake. Neither is open-ended. Then the ordinary calls: `launch`, `stake`, `beginUnstake`, `cancelUnstake`, `withdrawUnstaked`, `getReward`, `pull` to start a launch’s reward stream, and `claim` on the launch’s claim contract. Gas for these transactions is your own ETH. When sponsorship is enabled for your wallet and its first claim passes the fee, daily-budget and availability checks, Pines instead pays for `relayClaim`, with no wallet message or transaction approval. A later claim or a first claim declined for sponsorship follows the ordinary wallet transaction flow. Pines has no trading contract: Buy opens Uniswap’s own app, which asks for its own approvals and may ask for a Permit2 signature; that request is Uniswap’s, made after you leave Pines.

The contracts retain a typed-data signature for the legacy `claimFor` path: `ClaimIntent(bytes32 attestationHash,address ref,uint64 deadline)` under the EIP-712 domain `PinesAirdrop` version 4. The current sponsored-first-claim path uses `relayClaim` instead and asks for no such signature. Neither path lets its caller change the recipient fixed by the attestor.

Never: a Permit or Permit2 signature, setApprovalForAll, a transfer “to activate”, a console command, your private key or your recovery phrase. Anyone asking for those is not Pines.

## 11. Services involved

Netlify serves this site and holds its password. Railway runs the identity API (which also relays the enclave’s encrypted traffic), the read service, the media service and their PostgreSQL database. It also runs two private claim relayers and MongoDB, with a separate database for each relayer’s transaction records. The identity API decides sponsorship; each relayer holds its own gas-paying key, separate from the attestor and contract administrator. AWS runs the verification enclave, on an EC2 host in its Sydney region. DigitalOcean runs the monitoring server. Alchemy answers the chain reads the page makes, through a same-origin proxy on this site, so your browser never contacts Alchemy. Goldsky indexes the chain for the read service, server-side. Token art and metadata are pinned to content-addressed storage through the media service.

Plausible counts page views without cookies, through this domain, where a site enables it. Browser error reports go to our own monitoring server through this domain and carry no wallet address. A token page embeds a GeckoTerminal chart in a frame, so GeckoTerminal sees your address and browser when such a page loads. The buy dialog behind every Get button is KyberSwap’s swap widget: your browser asks KyberSwap’s aggregator API for quotes with the tokens and the amount, and sends it your wallet address when you press Swap, so it can build the route. Its chain reads go through this site’s same-origin proxy, and it loads its Ether icon from CoinMarketCap’s image host. Pairing a mobile wallet goes through the WalletConnect relay. Signing in with an email address, where a site offers it, goes through Privy, which creates an embedded wallet for you and holds the shares of its key. Adding funds by card, where a site offers it, opens Privy’s own on-ramp, where Stripe, MoonPay or Meld, not us, take the payment and check your identity. Asking for a link to finish on a computer, from a phone, sends your email address to Resend, our email provider, for that one message; we keep it in no database and no log, and Resend retains sent-message content, recipients and delivery logs for 30 days on its standard plans. Open and click tracking are off. If you also ask to hear when verification works on phones, Resend keeps a contact on that mailing list until you unsubscribe. The subscription provider sees your browser visits and the selected verification requests described above.

## 12. Limits

- Successful verifications have no wallet count limit. Per-IP, service-wide and enclave-capacity limits still apply.
- A verification is valid for 30 days, and binds the provider account to that wallet for the same 30 days.
- One claim per provider account per launch, enforced on chain by the nullifier.
- A drop is open for 1–7 whole days; a staked claim leaves after a 72-hour cooldown.
- A verification has 120 seconds from start to signed result, your review included.
- The enclave runs a limited number of verifications at once. When every place is taken, a new one is refused while its channel is set up, before anything is disclosed, and the extension says the verifier is busy; try again shortly.

## 13. What Pines does not do

- Take custody of anything, or hold a key that can move your assets.
- Ask for your private key, your recovery phrase, a password, a session cookie or an API key.
- Ask who you are: no name, no date of birth, no document.
- Read your conversations, files, prompts or payment details on any provider.
- Use the proven session to act as you — it never holds the credential.
- Sell personal information or build an advertising profile.
- Ask for an open-ended approval, or any Permit signature.
